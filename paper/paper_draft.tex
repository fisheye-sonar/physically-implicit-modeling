\begin{abstract}
A simulator summarizes an environment in a state that people can read and set. Generative models learn from observations and capture detail no one specified, but offer no straightforward way to set such a state. Combining the two would let us produce outcomes under interpretable conditions with the accuracy of a learned model. This requires \textit{editability}: the ability to set a model's latent state so that its next prediction follows from a target simulator state. To study when it arises, we fix the training setup and evaluation and vary only the environment. Our environments are Othello and Rayworld, our own world of moving discs rendered in 1D. Given observations paired with simulator states, a common approach is to decode the state with probes, then use them as editors to steer the model. We test this with linear and MLP probes, and train an inverse map on the same pairs in reverse as a third editor. In Othello, all three editors succeed on the standard game, yet small rule changes can toggle editability while the board stays decodable. In Rayworld, the probes steer the model only once their target is discretized, resembling the observation. The inverse map, however, succeeds from continuous positions throughout, even while predicting the latent state poorly. Our controlled comparison makes it measurable when a generative model can act as a simulator, a first step toward driving such models with interpretable states and rules.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\begin{figure}[b]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{figs/teaser.pdf}
\caption{We examine when a generative model's latent state can be set from interpretable simulator variables. (a) We train a generator on observations from an environment, then test whether a simulator's state can be transferred into it at inference. (b) In representation steering, we train a probe to read out simulator state, then invert it or follow its gradients to steer the latent state toward a target $s^*$. (c) Alternatively, we train an inverse map from simulator state to latent state. Neither approach is trained on the observations its edits should produce. See \cref{fig:experimental_setup} for evaluation details.}
\label{fig:teaser}
\end{figure}

A simulator represents an environment by holding a state, advancing it by a rule, and rendering it into observations. In conventional simulators, this state is explicit and interpretable, allowing it to be set while preserving commitments to that environment and its rules \citep{todorov2012mujoco}. Yet what these systems can express is constrained by what has been specified. Generative models, by contrast, learn from observations and can supply plausible details where that specification is incomplete \citep{brooks2024sora,bruce2024genie}. If an observer has seen only half of a room and then turns around, a generative model can propose what lies behind them, consistent with what they have already seen \citep{lu2025genex}. Their internal representations, however, typically offer no straightforward means of setting the environment's state \citep{shen2020interfacegan,tewari2020stylerig}.

Combining the two would let users test outcomes under interpretable conditions while drawing on details the simulator does not specify. Applications range from forecasting and planning to training agents \citep{lam2023graphcast,hafner2025dreamerv3}. We investigate one requirement for this through \textit{editability}: whether changing a model's latent representation produces the output changes expected from editing simulator variables. This is a question about the functionality we can access, without requiring that the model internally represents or advances state in the same way as a conventional simulator. Our experiments examine the effect of an edit on next-step predictions.

Whether a simulator variable is editable is not apparent from its decodability alone. A variable may be accurately decoded from a model's latent representations without the decoding probe providing a means of changing its effect on the output \citep{hazineh2023linear,canby2025reliable}. We ask instead which conditions make a variable editable. To avoid confounds from the architecture, training, or evaluation, we hold all three fixed and vary only the training environment. Changing the rules governing a variable, or the observations through which it is revealed, then isolates their effect on its editability.

We study two classes of environment: the game of Othello, where observations are move sequences and state is a discrete board configuration \citep{li2023othello}, and Rayworld, where moving discs are rendered into partial 1D observations. We vary the game rules in Othello and the observation process in Rayworld, and also compare continuous and categorical descriptions of the discs' state. Both environments have known ground truth simulators, so the correct response to each edit can be checked directly.

Given observations paired with their simulator states, a common approach is to train probes that decode the state from the model's latent representations, then steer those representations with the probes \citep{li2023othello,nanda2023linear}. We train linear and MLP probes and derive from each an editor that moves the latent state along the probe's directions. We call this representation steering. A failed steering edit, however, does not rule out that some other latent state produces the intended output. To test this, we train an inverse map on the same data, from simulator state to latent state, and overwrite the latent state with the map's output. If the inverse map succeeds where steering fails, we can still drive the model from outside with simulator states, even if the model does not represent them as the simulator does. We score each edit by whether it moves the prediction toward the edited simulator state, and whether the prediction stays accurate.

Across our variants, decodability alone does not predict editability. Every model decodes its state well, yet removing a single rule from Othello disables all three editors. In every Rayworld variant, the probe-derived editors fail on continuous positions while the inverse map succeeds. A model can therefore be driven from a simulator's state even when its probes cannot steer it.

Our main contributions are as follows:

\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{World Models and Simulators}

World models encompass several related ideas, from learned environments for agent training \citep{ha2018worldmodels} to video generators presented as world simulators \citep{brooks2024sora,bruce2024genie,nvidia2025cosmos}. Whether these models represent environment state, use it in prediction, or allow it to be set are distinct questions \citep{li2025definition}. We focus on the last: whether setting a model's latent state produces the observations expected from a target simulator state.

Evaluations of world models approach these questions from different directions. \citet{vafa2024evaluating} test whether a model recognizes which future sequences are possible from an environment state, regardless of how it was reached. For video generators, \citet{kang2025physical} find accurate physical predictions within the training distribution but poor extrapolation, while What-If World tests whether changing a physical variable in a prompt produces the expected consequences \citep{cai2026whatif}.

Making generation controllable often involves supplying additional structure. Trained conditioning interfaces let users specify aspects of the output \citep{zhang2023controlnet,wang2024motionctrl,nvidia2025transfer}, while geometric manipulation of latent features can guide camera motion without further training \citep{hou2026camera}. PhysDreamer instead uses generative priors to infer material properties for an explicit physical simulator \citep{zhang2024physdreamer}. These approaches make particular properties editable through conditioning interfaces or supplied geometry and physics, so the available edits depend on the structure provided. We instead investigate whether a model trained only to predict observations can accept simulator state edits through representations it has already learned.

\subsection{Probing and Steering Learned Representations}

A common approach to studying learned world models is to decode environment variables and then intervene on their representations to test whether the model uses them. In Othello, \citet{li2023othello} decode board states from transformers trained on move sequences and use probe gradients to guide predictions toward edited boards. Representing token ownership as mine/theirs permits linear decoding and edits through vector arithmetic \citep{nanda2023linear}, and similar interventions produce moves legal under edited chess boards \citep{karvonen2024chess}. Yet accurate board decoding can coexist with ineffective edits in late layers and shallow Othello models \citep{hazineh2023linear}. \citet{chawla2026metaothello} train one model on several rule variants and find that probes can guide edits across them, whereas we train a separate model per variant and hold the training and analysis fixed.

Decoding accuracy must be interpreted with care, since a probe can itself learn the structure being recovered \citep{hewitt2019probes}. Amnesic probing tests whether a model uses certain decoded information by removing it from activations and measuring the effect on behavior \citep{elazar2021amnesic}. In video encoders, \citet{joseph2026physics} edit motion representations and use a separate probe to check whether they now indicate the target direction. \citet{esmati2026invisible} study whether video-model activations distinguish physically plausible from implausible events. In Atari models, \citet{zhang2026probing} also show that interventions along probe directions change predictions. Because our simulators can be edited directly, we can instead compare a model's edited output with the output of the corresponding simulator edit. As in image editing \citep{shen2020interfacegan} and language-model steering \citep{zou2023repe,rimsky2024caa}, success also depends on preserving properties outside the target \citep{canby2025reliable}. Our metrics measure both the targeted change and prediction quality.

A direct mapping into latent space can also set a model's state, without first finding a steerable representation. StyleRig learns such a mapping from face parameters into a fixed StyleGAN, using editing and consistency losses \citep{tewari2020stylerig}. Closer to our setting, \citet{liu2026carriers} learn latent edits from paired factual and counterfactual activations and compare the resulting rollouts with simulator counterfactuals. Our inverse mapping is fitted only on ordinary state--activation pairs, and we test whether it produces the intended output when probe-derived editors cannot.

Causal abstraction tests whether interventions on a model's internal representations correspond to interventions on variables in a higher-level causal model \citep{geiger2021causal}, typically by swapping activations between inputs and, in distributed alignment search, learning the subspace where such swaps succeed \citep{geiger2025foundation,geiger2024das}. We share this intervention-based criterion, but our high-level model is the simulator we want to drive the model with, not a hypothesis about its computation. Our editors are fit only to paired latent and simulator states, without counterfactuals or optimization for intervention success, which lets us test whether decodable representations are also editable.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figs/setup_overview.pdf}
\caption{Overview of our evaluation procedure. The environment simulator renders state $s_t$ into observation $o_t$. The frozen generative model encodes the observations into latent state $z_t$ with $G_E$, and $G_D$ maps it to the next-step prediction $\hat{o}_{t+1}$, where $z_t$ holds the residual activations at every point and $G_D$ reads the last. We measure \textbf{decodability} by training probes to recover simulator state from the latent state. To measure \textbf{editability}, we edit the simulator state to obtain a target $s_t^*$, use an editor to produce a corresponding latent state $z_t^*$ and pass it through $G_D$ for the edited prediction $\hat{o}_{t+1}^*$. We compare this prediction with $o_{t+1}^*$, rendered from the edited state one step forward, using the unedited observation and prediction as references (\cref{eq:editability_formulae}).}
\label{fig:experimental_setup}
\end{figure}

Our experiments hold the model and its analysis fixed while the environment changes, so a difference between two rows of our tables is a fact about the environment. We use a common scheme for both environments, shown in \cref{fig:experimental_setup}. A simulator holds a state $s_t$ and renders it into an observation $o_t$, a move in Othello and a frame in Rayworld. The encoder $G_E$ maps $o_t$ and earlier latent states $z_{1:t-1}$ to $z_t$, the residual stream at every point, and the decoder $G_D$, the output layer, reads the last point to predict $\hat{o}_{t+1}$. An edit changes $s_t$ to a target $s_t^*$, an editor proposes a matching latent state $z_t^*$, and $G_D$ maps it to $\hat{o}_{t+1}^*$. The reference is $o_{t+1}^*$, rendered from $s_t^*$ advanced one step, beside the unedited $o_{t+1}$. We vary each environment along the axis it offers: the rules in Othello and what the observation reveals in Rayworld.

\subsection{Othello}
\label{sec:othello_experiments}
Othello is played on an $8 \times 8$ board. Players alternate placing tokens, and a legal move must enclose a line of opponent tokens, which then flip to the mover's color. We follow the setup of \citet{li2023othello}, whose generator draws every move uniformly from the legal set, so the optimal prediction is known exactly: a uniform distribution over the legal moves (\cref{app:predict_skill}). Following \citet{nanda2023linear}, instead of using black and white we describe the board as mine and theirs relative to the player about to move, the form in which it is linearly decodable.

The simulator state $s_t$ is the board after each move, with every cell being empty, mine, or theirs. An edit flips the color of one token after a real 20-move opening and is judged against the legal moves of the board before and after the flip, according to each variant's own placement rules. The flipped board need not be reachable by any legal game, a property the edits of \citet{li2023othello} share. We split our results by reachability in \cref{app:legal_vs_illegal_othello}.

\paragraph{Othello Variants.}
We introduce three variants of Othello. One modifies where a token may be placed, another removes the flipping of enclosed tokens, and the third does both. The two rules play distinct roles: placement keeps color relevant to predicting legal moves, while flipping makes the board hard to recover from the move sequence. In \texttt{adjacent-flip} a token may be placed anywhere adjacent to a token of its own color, including diagonally, with flipping active. Flips still change the legal moves but become rarer, at 0.27 flipped tokens per move against 2.2 in the \texttt{standard} game. \texttt{adjacent-noflip} uses the same placement rule with flipping off, so a placed token never changes color. \texttt{standard-noflip} keeps the standard placement rule without flipping. Every game then fills the same checkerboard in a different order, and a token's color is fully determined by the cell it occupies. \Cref{fig:othello_and_variants} illustrates the setup and its variants.

\subsection{Rayworld}
\label{sec:rayworld_experiments}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figs/rayworld_overview.pdf}
\caption{(a) In \texttt{standard} Rayworld, two discs move at constant velocity in a 2D frustum without colliding. Each frame is rendered into a 1D observation by casting rays into the frustum, shown stacked over time. (b) In \texttt{blink}, discs vanish for signaled periods of several frames, and (c) the \texttt{N-ray} family coarsens the observation by casting fewer rays. (d) The categorical target describes each disc by appearance bins, sets of positions that produce the same observation. See \cref{sec:rayworld_experiments}.}
\label{fig:rayworld_and_variants}
\end{figure}

Rayworld is a 2D world seen through a single fan of rays. An observer at the origin looks into a trapezoidal frustum in which two discs move at constant velocity for 40 frames. Trajectories in which a disc would hit a wall or the other disc are rejected, so the dynamics are exactly linear. Each frame is rendered into a 1D observation by casting 128 rays, spaced to cross the far edge of the frustum at equal intervals. A ray returns the reflectivity of the nearest disc it hits ($0.4$ or $0.8$ and fixed per disc) or $0$ if it hits neither, giving one noiseless pixel per ray. Depth is never observed directly. It enters only through the number of rays a disc covers, which falls with distance, and through occlusion.

Here $s_t \in \mathbb{R}^8$ holds the position and velocity of both discs and evolves as $s_{t+1} = A\,s_t$, where $A$ advances each position by its velocity, and the frame $o_t = h(s_t) \in [0,1]^R$ over $R$ rays is given by the rendering function $h$. The best possible prediction of the next frame averages $h(A\,s_t)$ over whatever the past frames reveal about $s_t$. Nothing here forces a predictor to represent the state itself, and whether it does is what the probes and editors investigate. We probe the state in the Cartesian coordinates $A$ acts in. An edit teleports a single disc, moving it to a random position inside the frustum that is clear of the walls and of the other disc, with both velocities left unchanged. Unlike an Othello edit, every edited state is a valid configuration of the world, with both discs inside the frustum and apart.

The description of state we probe is a modeling choice rather than a property of the environment. Othello's board is categorical and Rayworld's positions are continuous, so we also give Rayworld a categorical target for comparison. One option is a uniform grid over the frustum, with every bin labeled empty, bright disc, or dim disc. We instead let the observation draw the bins. Each disc lights a run of adjacent rays whose center follows its lateral position and whose length follows its depth, and only a finite set of runs is possible. Treating each run as a bin, two positions share a bin exactly when the disc looks the same, and we call the result the appearance target. An edit then moves a disc between bins, as an Othello edit changes one cell. We factorize the target into a center label and a length label per disc (15 and 5 classes on the \texttt{8-ray} variant) so the output size stays manageable at higher resolutions. Binning by appearance places the target in observation space rather than in positions. \Cref{app:rw_grid_ablation} tests that choice against evenly spaced bins, and we return to it in the discussion.

\paragraph{Rayworld Variants.}
We introduce two variants of Rayworld, each modifying what the observation reveals. In \texttt{blink}, a disc vanishes at random for 1 to 12 frames, with the frame before the blackout and its last hidden frame marked on an edge ray, so its position must be carried across the gap rather than read off the current observation. The \texttt{N-ray} family coarsens the observation by casting fewer rays, for $N \in \set{5, 8, 16, 128}$, to examine how the level of detail affects what a probe can find. The disc radius is doubled so a disc always covers at least one ray. Its \texttt{128-ray} member shares \texttt{standard} Rayworld's ray count and matches it on every continuous column, so the family's trends are an effect of ray count and not of disc size. \Cref{fig:rayworld_and_variants} illustrates the setup and variants.

\subsection{Model, Training, and Probes}
Every model is the same eight-block transformer of width 512 with about 25M parameters \citep{vaswani2017attention}, differing between environments only in its input and output layers. Rayworld uses linear input and output layers with a squared error loss, and Othello uses a token embedding and a softmax over moves with a cross entropy loss. To separate this interface from the environment, we also train an \texttt{8-ray} Rayworld model with Othello's interface, reading and predicting whole frames as tokens (\cref{app:tokenized_rw}). Each model trains for 780,000 steps at batch size 256 on 20M sequences, with a tenth held out for validation. The main results tables come from one seed per variant. To estimate variance we retrain every variant from two further seeds at a shorter budget of 512,000 steps, refit every probe and inverse map on each replicate, and report the spread in \cref{app:metric_spread}.

A decoding probe reads the simulator state out of the model's latent state. The latent state $z_t$ comprises the residual stream, the vector every block adds to and passes on, at every point \citep{elhage2021mathematical}. Its value after block $k$ is residual point $k$, with point 0 as the embedding. At every point we fit a linear probe, as single affine maps \citep{alain2017probes}, and an MLP probe with one hidden layer of 128 units, the shape used by \citet{li2023othello}. Both are fit on about 1.2M pairs of latent state and simulator state, with inputs standardized. Regression targets are fit by least squares and categorical targets by cross entropy. The targets are the mine/theirs board in Othello, the eight-dimensional state in Rayworld, and the appearance target of \cref{sec:rayworld_experiments}, whose probes use a larger corpus of 200,000 sequences to match their larger output.

\subsection{Editors}

The editors differ only in how they produce $z_t^*$. With $f_{\mathrm{Lin}}(z) = Wz + b$ the linear probe, $W^{+}$ the pseudoinverse of $W$, and $f_{\mathrm{MLP}}$ the MLP probe at the edited residual point,
\begin{align}
\text{PI:}\quad & z_t^* = z_t + \alpha\, W^{+}\big(s_t^* - f_{\mathrm{Lin}}(z_t)\big), \\
\text{GS:}\quad & z_t \leftarrow z_t - \eta\, \nabla_{z_t}\, \mathcal{L}\big(f_{\mathrm{MLP}}(z_t),\, s_t^*\big), \\
\text{IM:}\quad & z_t^* = g(s_t^*).
\end{align}

The first two editors steer $z_t$ through the same probes that decode the state, so their success or failure indicates what those probes have found. Pseudoinverse injection (PI) makes the smallest change to the standardized latent state that moves the linear probe's reading onto the target, scaled by $\alpha$ so that $\alpha = 1$ lands exactly, and writes once at a single residual point. Gradient steering (GS) instead takes 100 gradient steps of size $\eta$ on the latent state, minimizing the MLP probe's loss $\mathcal{L}$ toward the target while weighting entries that should not change by $0.2$. Following \citet{li2023othello}, it writes at a starting point and again at every later point.

The inverse map (IM) runs the probe in reverse, testing whether the model can act as a simulator even when steering fails. We fit $g$ from simulator state to latent state, an MLP of the same size and training setup as the forward probe, and at edit time overwrite the latent state with $g(s_t^*)$ at one residual point. To rule out memorization, we compare against overwriting with the mean latent state of the ten nearest training states, which IM beats by 53.6\% in Edit Index on average across Rayworld variants, while in Othello the nearest neighbors never successfully edit (\cref{app:inverse_nearest_neighbor}).

Each variant is scored on 1000 edit cases, each a held-out sequence with one variable changed at step 20. We skip cases whose edit leaves the observation unchanged and sweep each editor over residual points and, for PI and GS, step size.

\subsection{Metrics}
We introduce three metrics, one for decodability and two for editability, each defined relative to a reference so that environments with very different outputs can be compared directly.

We measure decodability with Probe Skill $= 1 - L_{\mathrm{probe}} / L_{\mathrm{trivial}}$, a baseline-relative skill score in the sense of \citet{murphy1988skill}, where $L_{\mathrm{probe}}$ is the probe's held-out loss and $L_{\mathrm{trivial}}$ that of a predictor that ignores the model. For continuous targets we use squared error with the training mean as the trivial predictor, so Probe Skill reduces to $R^2$. For categorical targets we use the error rate, with the most common class of each cell as the trivial predictor. We also fit the same probes to a randomly initialized model \citep{li2023othello} and to the raw observation sequences, separating what training put into the model from what a probe could extract regardless.

Editability requires two measurements, since an edit can implement the target change while degrading the rest of the output, or improve the output without implementing the change. Edit Index measures whether the edited prediction moves toward the edited world, and Edit Fidelity whether the output as a whole remains accurate. Edit Index compares $\hat{o}_{t+1}^*$ against both ground truths over only the part of the output the edit should change: the rays whose values differ between the two worlds in Rayworld, and the cells whose legality the flip changes in Othello. With $\operatorname{RMSE}_{\Delta}$ the error over that support, $d_{\mathrm{edit}} = \operatorname{RMSE}_{\Delta}(\hat{o}_{t+1}^*, o_{t+1}^*)$, and $d_{\mathrm{uned}} = \operatorname{RMSE}_{\Delta}(\hat{o}_{t+1}^*, o_{t+1})$,
\vspace{0.25em}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:editability_formulae}
\text{Edit Index} = \frac{d_{\mathrm{uned}} - d_{\mathrm{edit}}}{d_{\mathrm{uned}} + d_{\mathrm{edit}}}, \qquad
\text{Edit Fidelity} = 1 - \frac{\operatorname{RMSE}(\hat{o}_{t+1}^*, o_{t+1}^*)}{\operatorname{RMSE}(\hat{o}_{t+1}, o_{t+1}^*)}.
\end{equation}
Edit Index is $+1$ when the prediction matches the edited observation and $-1$ when it matches the unedited one, with outputs far from both near $0$. Edit Fidelity scores the whole output against the edited observation relative to the unedited prediction, so that $1$ reproduces it exactly, $0$ is no improvement, and negative values indicate a worse prediction. We report each editor at its highest Edit Index among settings with Edit Fidelity of at least $0$, falling back to the highest Edit Fidelity where none qualifies. Selecting on Edit Fidelity instead leads to the same conclusions (\cref{app:edit_fidelity_choice}).

\Cref{tab:decodability,tab:editability} report decodability and editability for every variant, from one training run each. Retraining each variant from two further seeds gives a seed standard deviation of at most $0.004$ for every trained probe's Probe Skill, and below $0.04$ for the Edit Index of every editor that lands. Cells whose spread exceeds $0.1$ carry a dagger, and all but IM's fidelity on \texttt{adjacent-flip} are failing editors whose selected setting flips between seeds at the fidelity cutoff (\cref{app:metric_spread}). Every model predicts its environment well before any probing or editing begins (\cref{app:predict_skill}).

\paragraph{Decodability.}
The simulator state is decodable in every variant, with the MLP probe reaching a Probe Skill of at least $0.88$ and the linear probe close behind, so what separates the variants is the baselines. In Othello, training adds something the baselines lack only when flipping is on. Once flipping is removed the board can be read off the move sequence itself, so the \texttt{adjacent-noflip} model only matches its observation baseline, and every probe on \texttt{standard-noflip} scores a perfect $1.000$, even on a randomly initialized model. In Rayworld, a randomly initialized model already comes within $0.03$ of the trained one under the MLP probe on every variant except \texttt{blink}, where a hidden disc must be carried forward rather than located in the current frame.

\paragraph{Editability.}
Every editor edits \texttt{standard} Othello, reproducing \citet{li2023othello} and \citet{nanda2023linear}, each carrying the predicted move distribution most of the way to the flipped board. Every change to the rules breaks this. With flipping off, no editor lands on either no-flip variant, even on \texttt{adjacent-noflip}, where legal moves still depend on token color. \texttt{adjacent-flip} is where the editors first come apart: IM still lands, while PI and GS reach at most $+0.35$ at low fidelity. 

In Rayworld this separation becomes the rule. On continuous positions, the probe-derived editors fail on every variant while IM lands everywhere, including two further variants in \cref{tab:additional_rw}. PI's write reaches its target in probe space, since at $\alpha = 1$ the linear probe reads the edited state exactly, yet the predicted frame does not move. A latent state that renders the teleported disc therefore exists and can be written, but not along any direction the probes provide. Discretizing the target changes this. It raises GS on the \texttt{128-ray} model, and as the rays coarsen both probe-derived editors rise steadily, to $+0.51$ and $+0.60$ at 5 rays. Since the number of bins falls with the ray count, this trend cannot separate coarseness from target size. At 8 rays, evenly spaced bins that ignore the observation also improve on continuous positions, but the appearance bins improve most (\cref{app:rw_grid_ablation}). Every conclusion above survives selecting each editor by its best fidelity instead (\cref{app:edit_fidelity_choice}), and \cref{fig:qualitative_edits} shows representative edits.

\section{Discussion}
Editability is sensitive to the environment in ways decodability does not reveal. In Othello, a single token's color stays editable under both flipping variants, although under the adjacency rule only the inverse map remains successful. Once flipping is removed, no editor succeeds. Without flipping, a token keeps its original color, so each color's count is fixed by the number of moves, and flipping one token yields a board no game can reach. Unreachability alone does not explain the failure, since under flipping rules the model accepts unreachable boards readily. Flipping two tokens of opposite color instead preserves the counts, and on \texttt{adjacent-noflip} the inverse map lands again, partly even on unreachable boards (\cref{app:legal_vs_illegal_othello}). The rules thus constrain which states can be written into the model, not only which can be reached. On \texttt{standard-noflip}, color is fixed by the square and is not a free variable at all, leading every tested editor to fail.

In Rayworld, what matters is how the state is described. The probes find no steerable direction in continuous positions, but find one once the target is discretized, and find the best one when its bins match what the observation distinguishes. The inverse map instead writes continuous positions at almost every residual point, and giving the model Othello's token interface leaves the outcome unchanged (\cref{app:tokenized_rw}). The inverse map thus lets us keep the simulator we prefer even where the model appears to have bypassed it. In Othello, by contrast, IM lands only at points 4 and 5 (\cref{app:im_editability_trends}), as if the model funnels its history through a board-like bottleneck that the Rayworld model never forms. Whether a model must form one depends on what the environment demands of prediction, resembling how a task may force a minimal sufficient statistic of the past \citep{shalizi2001computational}. Our findings do not settle this mechanism, but holding the model fixed while varying the environment turns it into a measurable comparison.

The inverse map's fit and its edits also separate in Rayworld, where it explains far less of the latent variance ($R^2$ of $0.32$ to $0.58$) yet lands everywhere. Every edit there targets a valid state like those the map was fit on, so the weak fit suggests that much of the latent variance has little bearing on the prediction. The edited predictions are visibly smoother than the model's own (\cref{fig:qualitative_edits}), consistent with $g$ writing a simplified latent that omits some of this variance.

\section{Conclusion}
We investigated when a generative model can be driven as a simulator by setting its latent state, and found that the answer depends on the environment in ways decodability does not predict. With the model, probes, editors, and metrics held fixed, a change to Othello's rules or to Rayworld's observation can switch editing of specific variables on or off while the state stays decodable throughout. Probe-derived editing is therefore not sufficient to decide whether a model can act as a simulator. Our editability concerns a single step, whereas a simulator also carries its state forward under consistent rules (see \cref{app:limitations} for further limitations). Rewriting the history lets an edit persist through a rollout (\cref{app:history_editing}), and the next step is to drive a model continuously from a simulator, or from different simulators at different times, and to discover which environments make that possible.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{app:limitations}
Our editability is a statement about only one step. A simulator also carries its state forward under consistent rules, and we test only whether a single write changes the next prediction as it should. Rewriting the history so that an edit persists through a rollout is one route, which we demonstrate in \cref{app:history_editing}, and extending the metrics to rollouts is the natural next step. Every model here is one transformer at one depth, so some of what we find may be a property of that architecture or that depth rather than of the environments, and recurrent or diffusion models may behave differently. The environments also differ in difficulty in ways we cannot fully control, and a harder environment may be less editable for reasons unrelated to how its state is represented.

\subsection{Predictive Loss of Trained Models}
\label{app:predict_skill}

% figure facts: paper/figs/predictive_quality/README.md (free-runs of 15 steps after 20 observed frames; two bench cases per panel).
Every model predicts its environment nearly as well as the environment allows, so none of our editing results can be blamed on a weak predictor. \Cref{tab:predictive_skill} places each model's held-out loss between two references. The trivial predictor ignores the history and always makes the same guess, the overall frequency of each move in Othello and the average frame in Rayworld. The Bayes floor is the loss of the best possible predictor, which knows the environment's rules and is limited only by what the history leaves uncertain. The Othello floor is exact, since the generator draws each move uniformly from the legal moves. In Rayworld, a sequence is fixed by the discs' starting positions and velocities, but different starting states can light the same rays, so the frames seen so far do not pin down the next one. The best predictor averages the next frame over every starting state consistent with those frames, weighted by how likely the generator was to draw it. We approximate this average with sampled starting states, dropping each one as soon as it fails to reproduce an observed frame. The floor rises as the rays coarsen and leave more of the state undetermined. Every model closes at least 99\% of the gap from the trivial predictor to its floor. \Cref{fig:rayworld_predictions,fig:othello_predictions} show this accuracy directly. Rayworld models rolled out on their own for 15 frames track the true continuation, with most errors a ray or two at a disc's edge, and the \texttt{blink} model accurately resumes a hidden disc after its blackout. On \texttt{5-ray} a rollout can keep a disc on the wrong ray for several steps. In Othello, boards shaded by the model's predicted distribution are nearly indistinguishable from boards shaded by the true legal moves.

\subsection{Metric Variance and Confidence Intervals}
\label{app:metric_spread}

The seed spread is small next to the differences between variants. We retrain every variant from two further seeds and compare the three members of each set at 512,000 steps, namely the two new models and the main run's own checkpoint at that step, each scored exactly as in the main tables. \Cref{tab:seed_spread} gives the standard deviation over the three seeds, and a 95\% confidence interval for their mean spans $\pm 2.48$ standard deviations.

No Probe Skill varies by more than $0.004$, and the Edit Index of every editor that lands varies by at most $0.034$. Each step of IM's rise as Rayworld's rays coarsen is at least seven standard deviations. Four cells exceed $0.1$ and carry a dagger in \cref{tab:editability}. Three are failing editors on the two adjacency variants, PI on both and IM on \texttt{adjacent-noflip}, and their spread comes mostly from selecting a setting on each seed rather than from the models. On these variants the writes that raise the Edit Index most also degrade the rest of the board, so the best setting with an Edit Fidelity of at least $0$ sits at that cutoff, and small differences between seeds decide which side of it a setting falls on. On \texttt{adjacent-flip}, for example, PI at the main run's setting reaches $+0.28$ and $+0.33$ on two seeds, but its Edit Fidelity is $-0.40$ on the third, which falls back to a weaker setting at $-0.12$. Holding the main run's setting fixed, the Edit Index of both PI cells varies by only $0.05$ to $0.07$. IM on \texttt{adjacent-noflip} stays unstable even then, at $0.32$, and on one seed no setting passes the cutoff, so it reports a write that barely moves the prediction. We keep the per-seed selection in \cref{tab:seed_spread}, since it is what rerunning the full pipeline would give. The fourth dagger, IM's Edit Fidelity on \texttt{adjacent-flip}, involves no selection. All three seeds write at the same point with an Edit Index of $0.62 \pm 0.03$, while the fidelity ranges from $0.40$ to $0.59$.

Outside the daggered cells, the seed means sit within $0.05$ of each Edit Index in \cref{tab:editability}, which reports the same runs at 780,000 steps. Edit Fidelity can differ by up to $0.26$ where the selected setting moves between the two checkpoints. PI on \texttt{standard} Othello, for example, is selected at a larger step size at 512,000 steps, with a mean Edit Fidelity of $0.48$ against $0.70$.

Refitting the probes on a fixed model matters less still. Refitting the linear probe with six to ten seeds, which change its initialization and held-out split, moves Probe Skill by at most $0.0007$ and PI's Edit Index by at most $0.016$ on \texttt{standard} Othello, the three \texttt{adjacent-flip} models, and the categorical target of the \texttt{8-ray} model. Refitting the inverse map ten times moves IM by $0.004$ on \texttt{standard} Othello but by up to $0.036$ on \texttt{adjacent-flip}, close to its seed spread there. The MLP probe behind GS was not refit.

\subsection{Matching GPT-style Prediction in Rayworld}
\label{app:tokenized_rw}

% prose facts: vocabulary datasets/discworld/dw-8ray/tokens/ (421 frames + UNK, research/findings/interface-ablation.md); 951 kept =
% bases["appearance-fac"].n_cases_kept of the token run's scores.json (pim/environments/discworld/token_bench.py drops pre == post frames);
% mean-frame MSE 5.73 vs 5.74 x 10^-3 from Table A1; probe gaps from the table below.
Othello and Rayworld models differ in their interface as well as their environment. The Othello model reads each move as a token and predicts a distribution over the next one, in the manner of a GPT-style language model, while the Rayworld model reads and predicts frames as vectors under a squared error loss. To make sure this interface is not what separates the two environments, we train an \texttt{8-ray} Rayworld model with Othello's interface. With fixed reflectivities and no noise, every ray takes one of three values, so each frame is one of a finite set of patterns. Every pattern that occurs in the dataset becomes a token, giving a vocabulary of 421 frames plus a reserved unknown token. The model embeds each frame token, predicts a softmax over the next frame, and trains with cross entropy, using the same sequences, architecture, and recipe as the \texttt{8-ray} frame model. Probes and editors act on the residual stream and carry over unchanged. Only scoring needs a choice, since the model outputs a distribution over frames rather than a frame. The frame-set reading follows Othello and compares that distribution against the frames of the edited and unedited worlds. The mean-frame reading averages the distribution into one frame and scores it exactly as the frame model is scored. The frame-set reading needs the two worlds' next frames to differ, which removes 49 of the categorical target's 1000 cases from the token model's rows.

The interface changes almost nothing. The token model decodes the state as well as the frame model, with every trained probe within $0.013$ of it (\cref{tab:tokens_decodability}), and its mean frame predicts as accurately (\cref{tab:predictive_skill}). Its editability also follows the same pattern under either reading (\cref{tab:tokens_editability}). On continuous positions PI and GS fail while IM lands, and on the categorical target the probe-derived editors improve while IM lands. The frame-set reading gives lower scores to the editors that land, since it credits only probability on the exact edited frame, and a prediction that places the disc one ray off earns nothing. Whatever keeps the probes from steering Rayworld's continuous positions, it is not the vector interface.

\subsection{Additional Rayworld Variants}
\label{app:additional_rayworld_runs}

% prose facts: renderer and arena from research/findings/smooth-ablation.md and observer-ablation.md; numbers from the table below
% and Table 1 (random-init linear skill: blink 0.608 -> 0.907 is the next largest gain after smooth 0.615 -> 0.962).
Two further variants ask whether the probes fail on continuous positions because the observation shows position too crudely. In \texttt{standard} Rayworld a disc lights every ray it covers at its full reflectivity, so a frame changes only when a disc's edge crosses a ray, and a model might predict well without ever tracking a precise position. The \texttt{smooth} variant shades each disc like a dome instead. A ray passing at distance $d$ from the center of a disc of radius $r$ reads the disc's reflectivity times $(1 - (d/r)^2)^2$, so every lit ray varies continuously with the disc's exact position, while the set of rays a disc covers is unchanged. The \texttt{obs5} variant keeps coarse rays but adds viewpoints. Five \texttt{8-ray} observers stand evenly spaced on a ring around a circular arena, and each frame joins their five views into 40 values. A disc hidden from one observer behind the other disc stays visible to the rest, so together the views pin down its position far better than any one of them. Each variant trains the same model with the same recipe on data of its own, and we probe the same continuous Cartesian state.

Both variants make position easier to read, and neither makes it easier to steer (\cref{tab:additional_rw}). On \texttt{smooth}, training lifts the linear probe from $0.62$ on a randomly initialized model to $0.96$, the largest gain of any Rayworld variant, so the model does learn a linearly readable position. On \texttt{obs5} an MLP reads position from the raw frames alone at $0.98$, and both probes on the trained model exceed $0.97$. Yet PI and GS fail on both variants, with Edit Indices between $-0.19$ and $+0.02$ at an Edit Fidelity of $0.02$, while IM lands on both at least as well as on \texttt{standard}.

The two variants rule out two simple accounts of the probes' failure. The failure is not due to a frame that reveals position too coarsely, since the smooth profile makes every lit ray track the exact position. Nor is it due to a single viewpoint that leaves position ambiguous, since five viewpoints pin it down. A clearer view of the discs improves decoding without handing the probes a direction that moves the prediction, and the inverse map writes the edited state on every Rayworld variant we trained.

\subsection{Inverse Mapping Trends Across Residual Points}
\label{app:im_editability_trends}

% reconstruction test: research/findings/inverse-probe.md, 2026-09-14 evening, Test 2 (L-oth-20m, 1000 canonical cases): g(unedited board)
% written with no edit, error against the pre-edit legal set relative to the model's own: 1.8-2.0 at points 4-5, 5-16 at points 0-3 and 6-8.
% Rayworld Edit Index / fidelity per point: the table below (IM at point 6 = +0.59 / 0.66).
In Othello the inverse map edits at only two residual points, and the way its fit changes with depth suggests why. \Cref{tab:im_by_point} follows the \texttt{standard} Othello and \texttt{standard} Rayworld models through all nine points. In Othello, the first five blocks look like a compression phase. The inverse map's $R^2$ climbs from $0.39$ at the embedding to $0.83$ at point 5, as the board comes to determine more and more of the latent state, then dips to $0.73$ at point 7. IM lands only at the top of this climb, at points 4 and 5, and at every other point its write leaves the prediction worse than no edit. A reconstruction test agrees. Overwriting the latent state with $g$ of the unedited board, which should change nothing, keeps the output's error within about twice the model's own only at points 4 and 5, and raises it to five to sixteen times the model's own elsewhere. We read this as a bottleneck. The board is a sufficient statistic of the history for the next move, since the legal moves depend on the history only through it, and with flipping it is a complicated function of that history. The model appears to assemble the board in its early blocks, hold it near points 4 and 5, and then expand it into the features of its prediction. A board written earlier meets blocks that are still gathering one from the moves, and a board written later lacks the detail those blocks have added since. The inverse $R^2$ alone does not locate the bottleneck, since it is higher at point 8 than at point 5 while IM fails there.

Rayworld shows no such bottleneck. The inverse map's $R^2$ is highest at the embedding, where the latent state is a direct function of the current frame, and falls to about $0.3$ from point 1 on, where it stays. Yet IM improves the prediction at every point, and its Edit Index climbs steadily to $+0.59$ at point 6, at an Edit Fidelity of $0.66$, and stays above $+0.54$ through point 8. No single depth holds a compact state that the rest of the network depends on, and the write works at most depths. The contrast fits what each environment demands of prediction. Othello's rules make the board both necessary and costly to compute, which gives a model reason to form it once and route everything through it, as a minimal sufficient statistic of the past \citep{shalizi2001computational}. Rayworld's frames already show most of the state, and its dynamics are linear, so its model faces no such pressure. The probe-derived editors follow the same split in \cref{fig:editability_over_res}. On Othello, PI and GS peak at point 4, just before IM, and fail from point 6 on, while on Rayworld they fail at every point.

\subsection{Appearance Bins Against Evenly Spaced Bins}
\label{app:rw_grid_ablation}

For the probe-derived editors, categorical targets help even when their bins ignore the observation, and they help most when the bins follow it. We compare the appearance target on the \texttt{8-ray} model against evenly spaced grids over the frustum coordinates, matched to it at 30 bins and pushed beyond it at 128, with every bin labeled empty, bright disc, or dim disc (\cref{tab:categorical}). Every one of these grids improves GS from the continuous position target's Edit Index of $-0.07$ at an Edit Fidelity of $0.12$, reaching $+0.35$ to $+0.39$ at Edit Fidelities of at least $0.33$. Labeled the same way, the appearance bins improve it further, to $+0.59$ at an Edit Fidelity of $0.56$, and their factorized form from the main tables also beats every grid. Most of the gain is therefore in binning at all, and the rest in choosing bins the observation already defines. PI moves the same way, albeit less cleanly. None of this tracks Probe Skill, since the grids decode far worse than the position target and still edit better than it. Snapping each position to the center of its appearance bin while keeping the regression read-out returns GS to $-0.05$ at an Edit Fidelity of $0.07$, the continuous result, even though the partition there is the one that works.

The inverse map gains only from bins that follow the observation. It rises from $+0.71$ on positions to $+0.88$ on the appearance bins, both at an Edit Fidelity of $0.73$, but reaches only $+0.64$ to $+0.73$ on the grids, at Edit Fidelities of $0.54$ to $0.64$. Unlike the probes, the inverse map needs no discretization to land, and it improves only when the bins describe what the observation shows.

\subsection{Comparing Inverse Mapping to Nearest Neighbor Replacement}
\label{app:inverse_nearest_neighbor}

An inverse map fit on about 1.2 million pairs could succeed by memorizing them, recalling the latent state of a training example whose simulator state lies close to the target. We test this against a lookup with nothing to fit. For each target state, the nearest-neighbor control finds the ten fit states closest to it, by Hamming distance between Othello boards and standardized Euclidean distance between Rayworld states, and overwrites the latent state with the mean of their latent states. It draws on the same fit set as the inverse map, is swept over the same residual points, and is scored the same way. To compare how well each map fits, we also predict the latent states of held-out examples from their simulator states and report each map's latent $R^2$ at the point where the inverse map edits.

The inverse map beats the lookup everywhere, and in Othello the lookup never edits at all (\cref{tab:im_vs_nn}). Its Edit Index is negative on every Othello variant, even on \texttt{standard}, where IM reaches $+0.81$ at an Edit Fidelity of $0.62$, and its writes leave the prediction worse than no edit. A board after 20 moves is rarely seen twice, and more than half of the flipped boards are ones no game reaches (\cref{app:legal_vs_illegal_othello}), so the nearest training boards are almost always other boards, and the mean of their latent states lands on none of them. In Rayworld, where nearby states produce similar frames, the lookup partly lands, from $+0.27$ to $+0.60$ at Edit Fidelities of $0.22$ to $0.54$, but it trails IM on every variant by $0.17$ to $0.26$ in Edit Index and at lower Edit Fidelity. The inverse map therefore does more than recall nearby examples. The latent $R^2$ also shows how poorly fit predicts editing. The lookup fits Othello's latent states better, at $0.73$ to $0.90$, than the inverse map fits Rayworld's, at $0.32$ to $0.58$, yet the lookup never edits Othello while the inverse map edits every Rayworld variant.

\subsection{Persistent Transformer Edits with History Rewriting}
\label{app:history_editing}

% source: paper/figs/history_rewrite/scores.json and README.md (make_figure.py on runs/noise_ablation/L-dw-noiseless-20m, cartesian block;
% the first 32 bench cases, one seed; IM at residual point 6; Edit Fidelity = 1 - the stored ratio). Edit Index at the edit frame / step 1 /
% step 14: single write +0.61 / -0.63 / -0.74 (fidelity 0.64); rewritten history + write +0.63 / +0.59 / +0.23 (0.64); rewritten history alone
% +0.65 / +0.57 / +0.21 (0.52). History-frame RMSE against the counterfactual render: rewritten 0.114, original 0.264.
Transformers make persistent edits hard, because they carry no state from one step to the next. At every step the model recomputes its latent state from the full observation history. An edit written at the last position changes the next prediction, but at the following step the model reads the original history again, and all that remains of the edit is the one predicted frame now appended to it. That frame shows the disc in its new place while every frame before it shows the disc on its old path, and the model sides with the history. On the \texttt{standard} Rayworld model, a single inverse-map write lands at the edit frame, at $+0.61$ with an Edit Fidelity of $0.64$ on the 32 cases we use here, yet one step later the rollout has largely returned to the unedited world, at $-0.63$. Scoring rollouts would therefore mix two questions, whether the model reads the written state and whether it carries that state forward against a contradicting history. The main paper asks only the first, and scores every edit on the next prediction.

History rewriting makes the history agree with the edit. The edited state keeps the moved disc's velocity, and Rayworld's dynamics are linear, so integrating backward from the target gives the counterfactual state at every earlier frame, with the other disc on its original path. We then rebuild the history one frame at a time. At each step the model reads the frames rewritten so far, we overwrite its latent state at the last position with $g$ of the counterfactual state, at the inverse map's residual point, and the model's prediction becomes the next rewritten frame. The first frame is kept, since nothing precedes it. The rollout then starts from the rewritten history, with or without the usual write at the edit frame. The simulator never renders an observation in this process. Every rewritten frame comes from the model, driven by written states alone.

The rewritten history holds the edit through the rollout (\cref{fig:history_rewriting}). One step after the edit frame its Edit Index is still $+0.59$, and fourteen steps later it is $+0.23$, where the single write stands at $-0.63$ and $-0.74$. The rewritten history carries the edit on its own, reaching $+0.65$ at the edit frame with an Edit Fidelity of $0.52$ and no write there, and adding the write barely changes the Edit Index, at $+0.63$, while raising the Edit Fidelity to $0.64$. The rewritten frames sit closer to the simulator's render of the counterfactual history than the original frames do, at an RMSE of $0.114$ against $0.264$, though they are visibly blurrier, being the model's own generations. These numbers come from 32 cases and one seed, so they illustrate the mechanism rather than measure it.

For a transformer, the history is part of the state. An edit persists only when the history agrees with it, and history rewriting achieves this with nothing but the inverse map and the model itself. It points toward driving a generative model continuously from a simulator, writing the simulator's state into the model at every step and using the model's own predictions as the observations, so that the simulator supplies the state and the model the detail. Two obstacles remain. The backward integration relies on Rayworld's simple, reversible dynamics, while Othello, for example, has no unique past, since many games reach the same board. The rewritten frames also drift from the true render, so errors may compound over longer horizons. A model that carries its state forward explicitly, as a recurrent model does, might avoid rewriting the history at all.

\subsection{Selecting for Edit Fidelity Instead of Edit Index}
\label{app:edit_fidelity_choice}

% prose facts: settings compared through pim.figures.tables.collect(select="index") against collect(select="fidelity") (2026-09-23):
% PI and GS mostly take smaller steps; IM's Edit Index moves by at most 0.10 (adjacent-flip +0.66 -> +0.56).
Our main tables choose each editor's setting by its Edit Index, among the settings that leave the prediction at least as accurate as no edit. The rule rewards the strongest edit that does no harm, but it could flatter an editor sitting right at that cutoff, or make our conclusions depend on where the cutoff lies. \Cref{tab:fidelity_selected} applies the opposite rule, choosing the setting with the highest Edit Fidelity outright and reporting the Edit Index reached there. This rule prefers accuracy to change. PI and GS mostly move to smaller steps, trading Edit Index for Edit Fidelity, while IM, which has no step size, moves by at most $0.10$ in Edit Index.

Every conclusion of the main text survives the change of rule. All three editors still edit \texttt{standard} Othello. On \texttt{adjacent-flip} only IM lands, and on the no-flip variants no editor does. In Rayworld, PI and GS fail on continuous positions in every variant, while IM lands in all of them and rises as the rays coarsen. On the categorical target both probe-derived editors again rise as the rays coarsen, and IM lands throughout.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{app:implementation}

% source (checked 2026-09-23): pim/environments/discworld/config.py + bigcorpus.py (sim flags per instance), pim/environments/othello/corpus.py
% and scripts/make_othello_edits.py (bench recipe), pim/models/transformer_l.py, pim/training/train.py (TrainConfig; every run's config.json),
% pim/probes/base.py + inverse.py (FIT_EPOCHS/LR/BATCH, RETRIEVAL_K; categorical recipe = discworld/arms.py GRID_PROBE_RECIPE),
% notebooks/master_eval.ipynb SETTINGS (ALPHA_REG, ALPHA_CAT, GS_LAYERS), metrics.jsonl elapsed_s of the main runs (training time).

\paragraph{Environments.}
The Rayworld frustum spans depths 3 to 12, and its half-width at each depth is half that depth. Each disc's initial depth is drawn uniformly, then its lateral position uniformly across the frustum at that depth, and it moves in a uniformly random direction at a speed drawn uniformly between $0.05$ and $0.12$ per frame. Discs have radius $0.5$, or $1.0$ in the \texttt{N-ray} family, which casts $N + 2$ rays and drops the two that run along the walls. In \texttt{blink}, after the first three frames each disc begins a blackout with probability $0.05$ per frame, lasting a geometric number of frames with mean 7, capped at 12. Othello games come from the generator of \citet{li2023othello} under each variant's rules. A player passes only when it has no legal move, and a game ends when neither player can move. Each edit recolors one occupied square outside the four central ones, and a case is rejected if the flip leaves the legal moves unchanged or empty. Training, probe, evaluation, and edit sequences come from disjoint seed ranges.

\paragraph{Models and training.}
The transformer is the minGPT model of \citet{li2023othello}, with 8 heads, learned absolute position embeddings, and dropout of $0.1$. It reads 39 frames in Rayworld and 59 moves in Othello, whose vocabulary has 61 tokens. We train with AdamW at a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and weight decay of $10^{-4}$, clip gradients at norm $1$, and hold the learning rate constant after 2,000 warmup steps. Since the schedule is constant, a main run's checkpoint at 512,000 steps is a fair member of its seed set in \cref{app:metric_spread}. Main runs use seed 0 and the replicates seeds 1 and 2. Each model trains on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 or RTX 5090, in 8 to 12 hours for Rayworld and 19 to 26 hours for Othello.

\paragraph{Probes and inverse map.}
Probes are fit at every position of each sequence, with an 80/20 split by sequence into fit and held-out sets. Linear regression probes are solved in closed form. The MLP probes, the linear categorical probes, and the inverse map train with Adam at a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and batch size 4,096, for 200 epochs, or 50 epochs on the larger corpus of the categorical Rayworld targets. The inverse map minimizes squared error in the standardized latent space. Its input is the eight-dimensional state in Rayworld, the one-hot mine/theirs board in Othello, and for the categorical target the one-hot bin labels together with both discs' velocities. The nearest-neighbor control averages the latent states of the 10 fit states nearest the target, by standardized Euclidean distance on continuous states and Hamming distance on categorical ones.

\paragraph{Editors and evaluation.}
Every editor writes only at the last position of the history. On continuous targets PI sweeps $\alpha \in \set{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5, 8, 12, 20, 35, 60, 100, 175}$ and GS sweeps $\eta \in \set{0.002, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.35, 0.5, 0.7}$. On categorical targets, Othello's included, PI sweeps $\alpha \in \set{0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 35, 60, 100}$ and GS sweeps $\eta \in \set{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.35, 0.7, 1.5}$. GS starts at residual point 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8, while PI and IM are swept over all nine points. Edit Index is averaged over cases. Edit Fidelity divides errors that are each averaged over all cases, rather than averaging a ratio per case.
